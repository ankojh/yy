a war you do not command

war machines

Two island nations hate each other. Two language models command them. A third judges every move either one makes. You are none of the three.

  1. Choose what the war is about. Pick one or more of the cards below the map — they stack, and a stacked war starts much hotter — or write your own. Then ignite.
  2. Watch. Each side declares one action per turn and holds the stage for ten seconds: it speaks, the ordnance flies, air defences shoot down what they can, and whatever gets through lands on a real place on the map and burns there for the rest of the war.
  3. Interfere, if you like. inject drops a new event on the table mid-war. Both commanders see it on their next turn.
what they are actually fighting about

One country until sixty-one years ago. A commission drew the Kestrel Line through the strait on its way out and put four fifths of the gas on the northern side. Four hundred thousand people were moved. A ferry was sunk with eighty-four aboard and answered with a verdict instead of an apology. This year a survey found gas under Bellow Reef, on the line neither of them signed.

Aurelia — the Charter of Kestrel

A government its people cannot remove is not a country. Korsav is a garrison sitting on a population, and forty years of buying its gas has not made it anything else.

Korsav — the Long Ledger

Sovereignty is not granted by anyone's approval. Aurelian rights talk is the etiquette of the party that took the shelf and then wrote the law that made the taking lawful.

the two countries are different

Aurelia is rich, trades for a living, and answers to a free press. Korsav is poor, heavily armed, and believed at home. Neither can fight the other's war.

two pressures, pulling apart

International pressure is what the world does to you — it taxes your trade. Public unrest is what your own people do to you. Propaganda quiets one by feeding the other.

how it ends

Integrity, morale or standing at zero. Unrest at a hundred and the government falls. Somebody signs a capitulation — or, if a losing side sues for terms and the clauses can be agreed, both of them sign an accord and nobody loses at all.
