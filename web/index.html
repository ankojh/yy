you chair the Meridian Council

war machines

Two island nations hate each other. Two language models command them and a third judges their moves. You lead the international body whose decision breaks the deadlock—and starts the war.

  1. Choose how the Council responds. The Council adopts a formal position on recent developments. One capital welcomes it, the other does not, and the crisis begins.
  2. Watch. Each side declares one action per turn and holds the stage for ten seconds: it speaks, the ordnance flies, air defences shoot down what they can, and whatever gets through lands on a real place on the map and burns there for the rest of the war.
  3. Answer the phone. When an island needs relief, funds, defenses, weapons, or diplomatic cover, it asks. You may approve or decline; you never choose a target, issue a combat order, or join either side.
what they are actually fighting about

One country until sixty-one years ago. A commission drew the Kestrel Line through the strait on its way out and put four fifths of the gas on the western side. Four hundred thousand people were moved. A ferry was sunk with eighty-four aboard and answered with a verdict instead of an apology. This year a survey found gas under Bellow Reef, on the line neither of them signed.

Aurelia — the Charter of Kestrel

Rich, trades for a living, answers to a free press.

A government its people cannot remove is not a country. Korsav is a garrison sitting on a population, and forty years of buying its gas has not made it anything else.

Korsav — the Long Ledger

Poor, heavily armed, believed at home.

Sovereignty is not granted by anyone's approval. Aurelian rights talk is the etiquette of the party that took the shelf and then wrote the law that made the taking lawful.

Neither can fight the other's war.

How it ends. A nation breaks when its infrastructure hits zero or public unrest reaches a hundred—or both sides sign an accord and nobody loses at all.
